Planetary orbits in our Solar System do not significantly decay on human timescales because they exist in an almost ideal conservative gravitational system where energy and angular momentum are nearly perfectly conserved. The apparent stability arises from fundamental physics principles combined with the specific conditions of our planetary system.

In an ideal two-body system governed solely by Newtonian gravity, planetary orbits would be perfectly stable ellipses that never decay. This is because gravitational forces are conservative—meaning no energy is lost from the system during orbital motion. The total mechanical energy (kinetic plus potential) remains constant, and angular momentum is conserved, resulting in closed, repeating orbits that persist indefinitely without spiraling inward.

However, real planetary systems experience several extremely weak dissipative mechanisms that could theoretically cause orbital decay over immense timescales:

**Gravitational radiation** represents the most fundamental decay mechanism predicted by general relativity. All orbiting masses emit gravitational waves, carrying away energy from the system. For the Earth-Sun system, this energy loss is so minuscule that it would take approximately 10^150 years for Earth to spiral into the Sun—many orders of magnitude longer than the current age of the universe (13.8 billion years) or even the expected lifetime of the Solar System. This timescale renders gravitational wave emission completely negligible for planetary orbital stability.

**Tidal interactions** between planets and the Sun do cause some orbital evolution, but the effect is extraordinarily slow for planets at astronomical distances. Unlike close binary star systems or moons orbiting very near their parent planets (like Mars' moon Phobos, which will crash into Mars in 30-50 million years), the tidal forces between the Sun and planets like Earth are extremely weak due to the vast distances involved. Tidal dissipation timescales for planetary orbits are measured in many billions of years, far exceeding other relevant timescales.

**Chaos and dynamical instability** actually pose a more significant threat to long-term orbital stability than dissipative mechanisms. The Solar System is mathematically chaotic with a Lyapunov time of 2-230 million years, meaning that small uncertainties in initial conditions grow exponentially, making precise long-term predictions impossible beyond about 100 million years. Numerical simulations by Jacques Laskar and colleagues show that Mercury has approximately a 1-2% probability of developing a dangerously eccentric orbit in 3-4 billion years, potentially leading to collisions with Venus, the Sun, or even perturbing Mars toward Earth. However, this represents chaotic scattering rather than gradual orbital decay, and the overwhelming majority of simulation outcomes show continued stability for billions of years.

The dominant factor affecting planetary orbits in the foreseeable future is actually **solar evolution**, not orbital decay. In approximately 6 billion years, the Sun will exhaust hydrogen in its core and enter the red giant phase, expanding dramatically and potentially engulfing Mercury and Venus. During this process, the Sun will lose about half its mass through stellar winds, which will actually cause the surviving planets' orbits to expand outward rather than decay inward. Neptune's orbit, for example, will drift from 30 to about 60 astronomical units from the Sun.

The key distinction is that orbital decay mechanisms (gravitational radiation, tidal dissipation) operate on timescales vastly longer than the Solar System's remaining lifetime, while the primary threat to planetary survival comes from stellar evolution processes that will fundamentally alter the system's structure long before any significant orbital decay occurs. Current observational evidence and theoretical models consistently show that planetary orbits remain effectively stable on timescales of billions of years, which explains why we observe a Solar System that has persisted for 4.6 billion years without catastrophic orbital collapse.
