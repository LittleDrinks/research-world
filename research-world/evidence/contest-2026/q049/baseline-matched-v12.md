Planetary orbits in our Solar System are stable over billions of years due to four key mechanisms that prevent catastrophic orbital decay. First, gravitational radiation effects described by Peters (1964) cause orbital energy loss through gravitational waves, but for planetary systems this effect is negligible with decay timescales exceeding 10^25 years—far longer than the Sun's remaining lifetime. Second, tidal dissipation between planets and the Sun does cause orbital decay, but only for very close-in bodies; for Earth-like distances, tidal orbital decay timescales exceed 10^12 years. Third, while the Solar System exhibits chaotic dynamics with a Lyapunov time of 5–10 million years (Malhotra 2001), this chaos operates within bounded limits that maintain overall orbital stability over the Sun's 10-billion-year lifespan. The characteristic orbital changes remain small relative to current semi-major axes. Fourth, during the Sun's red giant phase in approximately 6 billion years, mass loss will actually cause surviving planetary orbits to expand rather than decay, with outer planets drifting to roughly twice their current distances as the Sun loses about half its mass (NASA 2024). Mercury and Venus will likely be engulfed during this expansion, but Earth's fate remains uncertain. The combination of extremely weak gravitational radiation, minimal tidal effects at astronomical distances, bounded chaotic behavior, and future orbital expansion due to stellar mass loss explains why planetary orbits don't catastrophically decay on timescales relevant to the Solar System's existence.
